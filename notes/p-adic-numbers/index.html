Decimal expansions

Every real number can be expressed by a decimal expansion.

Most numbers have a unique expansion, but some have two different expansions:

\[ 0.999999999\ldots \;=\; 1.000000000\ldots \]
Why?

Why is $0.99999\ldots = 1$?

$0.999999\ldots = 1$
$1 - 0.9 = 0.1$ $1 - 0.99 = 0.01$ $1 - 0.999 = 0.001$ $1 - 0.9999 = 0.0001$ $1 - 0.99999 = 0.00001$ $1 - 0.999999 = 0.000001$ $1 - 0.999999\ldots = 0.000000\ldots$

What's going on here???

Common sense tells us that $\ldots 99999$ is not a number.

…but these “proofs” that $\ldots 99999 = -1$ make it feel like there's something there.

Two options

  1. Shrug your shoulders. Write this off as a curiosity and never think about this again.
  2. Keep an open mind. Embrace the mystery of the unknown. Follow me down this rabbit hole.

Dr. Hensel's wonderful numbers

\[\begin{aligned} x &= \ldots \vas{8424731}\\ &= \cdots + \vas{8}\cdot 10^5 + \vas{4}\cdot 10^4 + \vas{2}\cdot 10^3 + \vas{7}\cdot 10^2 + \vas{3}\cdot 10 + \vas{1}. \end{aligned}\]

The main objection to $x$ being a number is that this sum clearly does not converge to a real number.

In 1897, Kurt Hensel discovered that we can build a rigorous mathematical theory of an alternate numerical universe in which infinite sums like this do converge:

\[ \text{The } \vas{\text{10-adic numbers}}\ \ZZ_{10}. \]

The 10-adic numbers $\ZZ_{10}$

A 10-adic number is an expression of the form

\[ \cdots + \vas{a_4}10^4 + \vas{a_3}10^3 + \vas{a_2}10^2 + \vas{a_1}10 + \vas{a_0} \]

with digits $a_i$ satisfying $0 \leq a_i \leq 9$. We write $\ldots \vas{a_4a_3a_2a_1a_0}$ as a shorthand.

Addition and multiplication work just like you would expect.

Key observation

If $\vas{a}$ and $\vas{b}$ are 10-adic numbers, then the $n$th digit of $\vas{a + b}$ or $\vas{a\cdot b}$ only depends on the $k$th digits of $\vas{a}$ and $\vas{b}$ with $k \leq n$.

Zero divisors

An important property of $\,\RR$

If $mn = 0$, then $m = 0$ or $n = 0$.

$\ZZ_{10}$ does not have this property!

\[ b(b^2 + 1) = (\ldots 87918643\vas{2})\,(\ldots 21289062\vas{5}) = 0. \]
  • We call numbers with this strange property zero divisors.
  • Not obvious, but the existence of zero divisors in $\ZZ_{10}$ stems from the factorization $10 = 2\cdot 5$.

In other words, $\ZZ_{10}$ has zero divisors because $10$ is not prime.

From $10$ to $n$ to $p$

For any integer $n \geq 2$, positive integers can be written in base $n$ using the digits $0, 1, \ldots, n-1$:

\[ 7 = \vas{1}\cdot 2^2 + \vas{1}\cdot 2 + \vas{1} = \vas{111} \ \text{(Base 2)}, \qquad\quad 7 = \vas{1}\cdot 5 + \vas{2} = \vas{12} \ \text{(Base 5)}. \]

The $n$-adic numbers $\ZZ_n$ consist of all expressions of the form

\[ \cdots + \vas{a_4}n^4 + \vas{a_3}n^3 + \vas{a_2}n^2 + \vas{a_1}n + \vas{a_0}, \qquad 0 \leq a_i \leq n-1. \]
Primes to the rescue

If $n = p$ is a prime number, then there are no zero divisors in the $p$-adic numbers $\ZZ_p$.

Just as every number $n$ factors into a product of primes, every $n$-adic number system decomposes into a product of $p$-adics according to the factorization of $n$. \[ 10 = 2 \cdot 5 \,\,\Longrightarrow\,\, \ZZ_{10} \cong \ZZ_2 \times \ZZ_5 \]

This decomposition is (a fancy version of) the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

The $p$-adic prism

Following the dubious identity $\ldots 999999 = -1$ down the rabbit hole led us to the wonderful world of $p$-adic numbers.

  • Each $p$-adic number system is a parallel arithmetic universe to our familiar real numbers $\RR$.

We only get a complete picture by considering $\RR$ together with all the $p$-adics.
